  1. 1874

    The Old Mint was completed in 1874 at a cost of about two million dollars, to the design of Alfred B. Mullett, Assistant Supervising Architect of the Treasury: four wings around an open central courtyard on a concrete foundation, two full floors over a street-level ground floor, with partial attics under the east and west gables. It served at once as foundry, fortress, factory and ornamented offices, and is often called the last major work of classical revival architecture built in America.

    88 Fifth Street, The Old Mint revised mitigated negative declaration, SF Planning Dept., 2005
  2. 1900

    Built.

  3. April 18, 1906

    The most prominent building in the area to survive the fire intact, with thick stone and brick walls, cast iron fire shutters, internal fire suppression reservoirs and a workforce that put out every fire that entered the building.

    Central SoMa Historic Context Statement, adopted 2016
  4. 1937

    One of only two Treasury Section of Fine Arts commissions completed in San Francisco: a series of bronze eagle reliefs by Albert Stewart, of which one survives.

    San Francisco New Deal Historic Context Statement, adopted 2023
  5. 1937

    The building lost its purpose in 1937, when the new San Francisco Mint opened on Hermann Street near Market and Duboce. Other uses followed; it held a museum from 1973 until 1994, and federal offices alongside, and stood empty by 2005. The Historic American Buildings Survey documented it in 1962.

    88 Fifth Street, The Old Mint revised mitigated negative declaration, SF Planning Dept., 2005
  6. 1958

    Architects Mario Ciampi and Allyn Martin presented plans to redevelop the site as 'Gold Plaza', an urban park surrounded by modern office towers, with only the Mint's columns left standing. The opposition the plan aroused led to the Mint's designation as a National Historic Landmark in 1961.

    Central SoMa Historic Context Statement, adopted 2016

Early Settlement Era Styles Historic Context Statement (2025)

StyleGreek Revival
Year built as surveyed1874

The Old United States Mint, San Francisco Landmark No. 236 — the statement's chosen exemplar of Greek Revival in the Bay Area, and the style at its most legible: large columns and pediments, often with elaborate friezes, and often intact.
